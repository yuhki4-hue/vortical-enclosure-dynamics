\section{Physics Describes Only the Past}

\label{sec:past}

The preceding discussion suggested that divergence may not be a mere failure of theory,
but a signal indicating that theory is approaching a boundary.

To understand this possibility,
we must first examine the structure on which physical theory is built.

Modern physics consists of multiple frameworks:
classical mechanics, relativity, quantum mechanics, and quantum field theory.
Each applies to different scales and conditions,
yet all share a common structure.

They assume that time and space are given.

Physics takes a state defined at a moment in time
and describes how it evolves.
Or it defines a field in space
and describes its behavior through equations.

In all cases,
the object of description is something that already exists.

Particles, fields, energy, spacetime---
these are given in advance,
and theory describes their relations and changes.

This structure is so natural that it often goes unnoticed.

Yet it has a crucial implication.

\medskip
\begin{quote}
Physics describes only the past.
\end{quote}
\medskip

The past here does not mean what has elapsed in time.
It means what has been fixed into a form that can be described.

What can be observed, recorded, and expressed in equations
is always something that has already been stabilized as structure.

Physical theory takes these stabilized structures as input
and describes the relations between them.

Even when predicting the future,
the prediction proceeds from initial conditions---
information that has already been fixed.

In this sense,
physics always operates within the domain of the past.

This structure is not accidental.

Physical theory presupposes several basic elements:
time, space, states, and observable quantities.

These are not derived within theory;
they are presupposed.

All equations, symmetries, and conservation laws
are defined on the basis of these given structures.

From this perspective,
physics consistently describes the domain after generation has occurred.

What, then, lies outside this domain?

A domain where time has not yet acquired meaning.
Where space does not yet hold.
Where states are not defined,
and no observable quantities exist.

How does theory respond to such a domain?

The answer may be divergence.

Divergence appears precisely when theory is pushed beyond the domain
in which its presupposed structures remain valid.

Equations no longer yield finite values.
Quantities become undefined.
The framework itself ceases to function.

This is not merely computational failure.

It is the response of theory
when it approaches the limit of its own conditions of applicability.

\medskip
\begin{quote}
Physics does not describe existence.\\
It describes the structure after generation.
\end{quote}
\medskip

From this view,
divergence is not a defect of theory,
but an indicator of its boundary.

Beyond that boundary lies a domain
that cannot be described within the existing framework.

Not merely unknown,
but in principle indescribable.

The next section examines the nature of this boundary more closely.

\subsection*{Supplement: What Is Pastification? --- Physics as Texture}

Let us restate the concept of \textit{pastification} more concretely.

The past is not merely what has elapsed in time.
It is what can be handled as a tangible form.

Color, temperature, weight, sensation---
all arise from micro-level details accumulating and being compressed at some scale.

A continuous physical quantity called wavelength rises up as the color red or blue.
The microscopic structure of woven thread appears as the tactile quality of smooth or soft.

In neither case do the raw details appear directly.
They are compressed and crystallized as texture,
and only then perceived as quality.

Symmetry and conservation laws in physics share this structure.
The accumulation of individual relations appears,
viewed from a certain distance, as an invariant structure.
It is an integral of relations---a texture born through compression.

In this sense, the past is the accumulated crystallization of relations as texture.

\medskip

What, then, is the \textit{pastification} of the future?

The future in principle contains possibilities that can diverge without limit.
These cannot be handled as they are.

When physics predicts the future,
it extracts from this diverging possibility space
only the quality that can be handled as texture through computation,
and places it on the same footing as the past.

Pastification of the future is, in other words,
the operation by which endlessly expanding possibilities are crystallized into texture.

And the mathematical procedure of that operation is renormalization.

Renormalization does not eliminate divergence.
It extracts micro-scale information as texture at a coarser scale---
compressing it into a form that can be handled as texture.

That is why renormalized theory describes reality well:
because it has been compressed consistently as texture.

\medskip

From this perspective, the meaning of the \DH{} becomes sharper.

As one approaches the horizon, the very foundation for compression is lost.
Structure that could be extracted as texture disappears.
Texture can no longer be constituted.

That is what appears as divergence.

The \ungenerated{} domain is the domain prior to the emergence of texture.
Not even the individual points of pointillism yet exist---
neither color nor tactile quality has yet emerged.

\medskip
\begin{quote}
Physics holds only within the world of texture.\\
Of what lies before it, nothing can be said.
\end{quote}
