## Chapter 1

## The Unease of Renormalization

Modern physics has achieved remarkable success.  
Quantum field theory in particular stands out for its precision, showing extraordinary agreement with experimental results.

Yet within it, there remains a peculiar operation that has long gone unexplained.  
That operation is renormalization.

As one proceeds through quantum field theory calculations, infinities frequently appear.  
The self-energy of particles, correction terms for interactions, vacuum fluctuations—  
these diverge mid-calculation, refusing to yield finite values.

From a mathematical standpoint, this is a clear breakdown.  
A theory meant to describe finite physical quantities returns infinity instead.

Yet physics does not stop here.

Divergent quantities are not simply left as they are.  
They are absorbed into the redefinition of observable quantities, emerging as finite values.  
This operation is renormalization.

Remarkably, the results obtained through this procedure agree with experiment to extraordinary precision.  
The anomalous magnetic moment of the electron, scattering cross-sections—renormalized theory describes reality with astonishing accuracy.

Here lies a peculiar situation.

The theory breaks down once under infinity.  
Yet after appropriate processing, it again describes reality correctly.

At this point, a question arises.

---

Why is such an operation permitted?  
Why can a theory containing divergences continue to describe reality?

---

This question is rarely pursued in depth.  
Renormalization has been accepted as a procedure that works, and its mathematical justification and formal organization have advanced.

Yet its physical meaning is not necessarily clear.

What is renormalization actually doing?  
Why do infinities appear?  
And why does removing them yield the correct answer?

No unified understanding of these questions has been given.

In fact, the more precise statement is this:

---

Renormalization succeeds.  
But why it succeeds is not understood.

---

This unease is not merely a technical problem.  
It may well concern the structure of theory itself.

Is divergence truly an error to be removed?  
Or is it a symptom of theory touching something else entirely?

If the latter, renormalization is not a mere correction operation.  
It may be converting boundary contact into another form.

This volume takes that unease as its starting point.

Rather than treating renormalization as a problem,  
it reframes it as a structural clue.

From this vantage, divergence becomes not mere breakdown,  
but a kind of contact signal.

And this reinterpretation extends beyond quantum field theory.

The Planck scale, the speed-of-light limit, singularities,  
and the incompatibility of gravity and quantum theory—  
the various unsolved problems of modern physics may share a common structure.

That structure is the appearance of a descriptive boundary—
the point where a difference-based description fails to convert
the absence of difference into an ordinary observable state.

This volume calls that boundary the **Differential Horizon**.

---

Let us begin with this unease.

Is divergence truly something to be removed?  
Or is it touching something?

## Chapter 2

## Is Divergence a Failure?

The previous chapter identified the unease inherent in the operation of renormalization.  
A theory breaks down once under infinity, yet through subsequent redefinition it describes reality accurately.

Implicit in this is one assumption.

---

Divergence is something to be removed.

---

Mathematically, this assumption is natural.  
Since a theory deals with finite physical quantities, infinity must be excluded.

But is this assumption truly self-evident?

Is divergence merely a computational error?  
Or is it a symptom of theory touching something?

This question is not limited to quantum field theory.

At black hole singularities, curvature diverges.  
Gravitational fields become infinitely strong, and existing physical laws lose their meaning.

At the Planck scale, the continuity of spacetime itself is thought to break down.

These are typically treated as the limits of applicability of theory—  
the point beyond which a different theory is needed.

But the perspective can be shifted.

These divergences may not be mere failures.  
They may be pointing toward a common structure.

Divergence in quantum field theory.  
Singularities in gravity.  
Breakdown at the spacetime limit.

All appear under a kind of extreme approach.

---

When theory approaches a certain boundary, divergence appears.

---

If so, divergence is not an error.  
It is a signal showing that theory is reaching toward that boundary.

The meaning of divergence then changes fundamentally.

It is not an anomaly to be removed,  
but information that cannot be ignored.

The equations are not broken.  
They are approaching a domain they cannot describe.

Crucially, this indescribable domain is not merely unknown.  
It may be a domain that existing frameworks are in principle incapable of describing.

For all current physical theories share a common premise:  
that change is described within a given framework of time and space.

Under this premise, all phenomena are treated as things that already exist.

But if the place where divergence appears is a boundary where that premise itself fails to hold,  
the situation is entirely different.

There, the framework of time and space itself loses meaning.

That existing theories return divergence when approaching such a domain

It is not error but appropriate response.

Divergence is not the failure of theory.

From this view, the meaning of renormalization also changes.

Renormalization does not remove divergence.  
It converts the signal generated by boundary contact  
into a form that can be handled within the theory.

This reinterpretation remains a hypothesis.  
Yet it holds the possibility of grasping multiple distinct phenomena from a single perspective.

Divergence, singularities, the Planck scale, the speed-of-light limit—  
these may not be separate problems

## Chapter 3

## Physics Describes Only the Past

The preceding chapters raised doubts about treating divergence as mere failure.  
If divergence is a signal indicating boundary contact,  
the next question is: what is that boundary?

To address this, we first need to examine what structure current physical theory rests upon.

Modern physics is built from diverse theories.  
Newtonian mechanics, relativity, quantum mechanics, quantum field theory.  
Each handles different scales and conditions, yet all share a common framework.

That framework is the assumption that time and space are given.

Physics takes a state at some moment in time  
and describes how it changes over time.

Or it describes how a field defined in space  
behaves according to some equation.

In both cases, the object of description is always something that already exists.

Particles, fields, energy, spacetime—  
these are given in advance, and their behavior is described.

This structure is so natural that it normally goes unnoticed.

Yet it carries one important feature.

---

Physics describes only the world that has already come to be.

---

The word "past" here does not mean merely what has elapsed in time.

Rather, it means what has been fixed in a form that can be described.

What can be observed, recorded, and handled in equations—  
all of this is a kind of settled state.

Physical theory takes these settled states as input  
and describes the relations and changes between them.

In this sense, physics always deals with the past.

Even an equation predicting the future  
departs from initial conditions—information already settled.

Its predictions are therefore merely extensions within a settled framework.

This structure is deeply bound to symmetry and conservation laws.

Conservation of energy and conservation of momentum are derived from symmetries of time and space.

And those symmetries are defined on spacetime that already holds.

Conservation laws, too, have meaning only within the post-generated world.

Viewed this way, current physical theory consistently handles the same domain:  
the structure after generation has completed.

What of what comes before?

A domain where time and space have not yet taken on meaning.  
A domain where states are not settled and have no describable form.

How does current physics respond to such a domain?

The answer may be the divergence seen in the preceding chapters.

Equations cannot describe that domain directly.  
But as one approaches it, calculation breaks down.

Infinity appears, and quantities become undefined.

This may not be mere computational failure.

It may indicate that theory is touching the boundary between the domain physics can handle  
and the domain it cannot.

---

Physics does not describe existence.  
It describes the structure after generation.

---

From this view, divergence and singularities are understood not as theoretical defects  
but as indicators of the limits of applicability.

And beyond those limits lies a domain not yet described.

It is not merely unknown.  
It is a domain that cannot in principle be described within existing frameworks.

The next chapter makes the character of this indescribable domain more explicit.

It is not mere blank space  
but a domain in which generation has not yet occurred.

### Supplement: What Is Pastification? — Physics as Texture

Let us restate the concept of **pastification** more concretely.

The past is not merely what has elapsed in time.  
It is what can be handled as a tangible form.

Color, temperature, weight, sensation—  
all arise from micro-level details accumulating and being compressed at some scale.

A continuous physical quantity called wavelength rises up as the color red or blue.  
The microscopic structure of woven thread appears as the tactile quality of smooth or soft.

In neither case do the raw details appear directly.  
They are compressed and crystallized as texture, and only then perceived as quality.

Symmetry and conservation laws in physics share this structure.  
The accumulation of individual relations appears, viewed from a certain distance, as an invariant structure.

It is an integral of relations,  
a texture born through compression.

In this sense, the past is the accumulated crystallization of relations as texture.

---

What, then, is the pastification of the future?

The future in principle contains possibilities that can diverge without limit.  
These cannot be handled as they are.

When physics predicts the future,  
it extracts from this diverging possibility space  
only the quality that can be handled as texture through computation,  
and places it on the same footing as the past.

Pastification of the future is, in other words,  
the operation by which endlessly expanding possibilities are crystallized into texture.

And the mathematical procedure of that operation is renormalization.

Renormalization does not eliminate divergence.  
It extracts micro-scale information as texture at a coarser scale—  
compressing it into a form that can be handled as texture.

That is why renormalized theory describes reality well:  
because it has been compressed consistently as texture.

---

From this perspective, the meaning of the Differential Horizon becomes sharper.

As one approaches the horizon, the very foundation for compression is lost.  
Structure that could be extracted as texture disappears.  
Texture can no longer be constituted.

That is what appears as divergence.

The un-generated domain is the domain prior to the emergence of texture.  
Not even the individual points of pointillism yet exist—  
neither color nor tactile quality has yet emerged.

Physics holds only within the world of texture.  
Of what lies before it, nothing can be said.

## Chapter 4

## Why the Un-generated Cannot Be Handled

The previous chapter showed that current physical theory consistently describes post-generated structure.

From this, a natural question arises.

---

Why can the domain prior to generation not be described?

---

Simply put, it may merely be unknown.  
One might expect that a more advanced theory will eventually describe it.

But what is at issue here may not be mere unknownness.

It concerns the very premises on which current theory depends.

Physical theory always presupposes several basic structures.  
Time, space, states, observable quantities—  
these are given as the starting point of theory, and laws are defined on them.

But the concept of the un-generated fits none of these presuppositions.

If time has not yet come to hold, the question "when" has no meaning.

If space has not yet come to hold, the question "where" does not arise.

If states have not settled, quantities cannot be defined.

If observable quantities do not exist, theory has no object.

From this it is clear that the un-generated domain is not merely unknown,  
but a domain to which the descriptive form of current theory is simply inapplicable.

Crucially, this is not a technical difficulty.

However precise a theory one constructs,  
as long as that theory presupposes structures like time and space,  
it cannot reach the un-generated domain.

This is not the incompleteness of theory  
but a constraint imposed by the very conditions for theory's holding.

---

The un-generated is not "not yet described."  
It does not fit the form of description at all.

---

At this point, the significance of divergence becomes clearer.

Theory cannot handle the un-generated domain directly.  
But it can approach its boundary.

And at the limit of that approach,  
quantities diverge and equations break down.

This is not coincidence.

It shows that a clear discontinuity exists  
between the domain that can be described  
and the domain that cannot.

This discontinuity is not a mere boundary line.  
It may appear rather as a transition region of finite thickness.

For physical theories are always built within finite scales and precision.

The un-generated and the generated are therefore not cleanly separable.

Between them exists a transition band accompanied by fluctuation.

Within this transition band,  
theory gradually loses its domain of applicability  
and finally shows its limit as divergence.

Here, naturally, the following view emerges.

---

Divergence is not the reaching of the un-generated domain.  
It is contact with what lies just before it.

---

This contact does not enable direct description.  
But it cannot be denied either.

Theory touches, responds, then breaks down.  
The manner of that breakdown itself may indirectly indicate the character of the boundary.

In this way, the un-generated domain,  
though directly indescribable,  
appears within theory only through its boundary.

The next chapter defines this boundary clearly.

It is introduced not as an abstract limit  
but as a structure with physical meaning.

This volume calls it the Differential Horizon.

Physics operates inside the horizon.

## Chapter 6

## Two Paths of Approach

The Differential Horizon cannot be reached directly.  
But it can be approached.

This approach takes two broad forms.

They correspond to what appear to be entirely different theoretical frameworks.

One is approach through spatial convergence.  
The other is approach through temporal convergence.

---

### Spatial Convergence

When physics proceeds to ever smaller scales,  
we enter the quantum domain.

Particles behave like points,  
fields fluctuate strongly,  
and position and momentum can no longer be simultaneously defined.

As scale is reduced further,  
theory gradually loses its stability.

Quantum field fluctuations increase,  
and calculations come to contain divergences.

Finally, at the Planck scale,  
the continuity of spacetime itself is thought to fail.

There, concepts like distance and position  
no longer carry clear meaning.

Space ceases to exist as a definable structure.

---

Reducing scale is approaching the Differential Horizon.

---

This path is described by quantum theory and quantum field theory.  
There, divergence appears frequently,  
and the operation of renormalization becomes indispensable.

In this sense, quantum theory  
serves as one path of approach toward the horizon.

---

### Temporal Convergence

The other path appears through pushing velocity to the limit.

In relativity,  
as an object's velocity approaches the speed of light,  
the passage of time slows.

At the limit, at the speed of light,  
time stops.

At that point, for a moving object,  
the passage of time can no longer be defined.

Simultaneously, spatial lengths contract,  
and distances along the direction of motion tend toward disappearance.

Here too,  
concepts like time and space lose meaning at the limit.

---

Approaching the speed of light is approaching the Differential Horizon.

---

This path is described by relativity.  
There, divergence appears in a different form.

Energy diverges toward infinity,  
and a body with finite mass cannot reach the speed of light.

This is not merely a constraint on motion.  
The structure of theory itself does not permit reaching it.

---

### Two Paths, One Boundary

These two paths are normally treated as separate theories.

Quantum theory handles microscopic scales;  
relativity handles high-velocity motion and gravity.

The two are not unified,  
and their connection is regarded as one of the major unsolved problems of modern physics.

Yet viewed from the perspective of the Differential Horizon,  
these are not different theories.

---

They are different paths of approach to the same boundary.

---

The path of approach through scale reduction.  
The path of approach through increasing velocity.

Both ultimately head toward a domain  
where time and space lose meaning.

And at their limit, divergence and breakdown appear.

This convergence is not coincidence.

It shows that both are approaching the same structure.

Quantum theory and relativity are not in opposition.  
Each is approaching a different aspect of the Differential Horizon.

---

Not different theories.  
Different modes of approach.

---

From this view,  
the problem of quantum gravity takes on new meaning.

It is not simply a matter of integrating two theories.  
Rather, it is a matter of understanding the structure by which two paths of approach converge on the same boundary.

The next chapter more clearly redefines the divergence appearing at the limit of this approach.

It is interpreted not as theoretical breakdown  
but as boundary contact.
